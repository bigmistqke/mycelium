A rejected word cannot separate two senses, so half the rejections were wrong

Cleaning the corpus against the new dictionary turned up more about the dictionary than about the corpus. Two of the eight rejections were wrong, and wrong in a way that only appeared once we ran them against real documents.

scaffolding was rejected in favour of harness. Across the whole corpus it never once meant harness: every use meant generating an initial skeleton of files, which is a different thing. Zero real violations from a rejection that looked obvious when written.

tooling was rejected for the same reason and fared little better. Ten uses, of which two meant the harness and seven meant software in general — an external skill, a headless browser, someone else's program that reads the plans. A checker enforcing that rejection would have produced seven false reports for two true ones.

The cause is structural rather than careless. A rejection matches spelling, and these words carry two senses each. ASD-STE100 avoids this by fiat: its dictionary allows one meaning and one part of speech per word, and a word that will not fit is simply not in the language. That works for aircraft maintenance procedures and does not work for prose about software, where tooling has a perfectly good ordinary meaning that the project has no business banning.

So the distinction survives as a rule rather than a rejection: say harness when you mean this project's own instructions and commands, marked as something a machine cannot decide. That is what the machine-checkable field is for, and this is the first entry where the answer had to be no for a reason other than difficulty. Six rejections remain, all of them abbreviations or single-sense words, which is the shape a rejection can actually carry.
