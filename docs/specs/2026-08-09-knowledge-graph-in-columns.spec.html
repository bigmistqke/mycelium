Days in columns, so the knowledge graph reads as a sequence rather than a mesh draft 2026-08-09

The knowledge graph draws 565 nodes with a force layout, which settles into a shape nobody chose. Reading it means untangling it first, and the arrangement carries no meaning a reader can rely on: two nodes near each other are near because the simulation put them there.

The axis it needs already exists. Every node carries its day in its filename, and knowledge accumulates through time, so the corpus records the one ordering that is true of it and the drawing ignores that ordering entirely.

The canon chain answers the same question with columns, but it earns them: an axiom narrows an axiom, a behaviour narrows a behaviour, a declaration cites a behaviour, and no edge in that vocabulary can skip sideways. Nothing about the knowledge graph's eight relations says the same.

Counting decides how far the comparison carries. Of 629 edges between knowledge nodes, 502 join two nodes from the same day and 127 cross days, 85 pointing back and 42 forward. Twenty days hold the whole graph, and the busiest holds 82 nodes.

A day is a partition, not a rank

Four fifths of the edges live inside one day. Making a day a column and stopping there would leave those edges doubling back inside a rank, which is the fault columns exist to remove, and twenty tidy columns would each hold a small mesh.

So the drawing gets two levels. Days run left to right as the outer axis, and each day's own nodes rank into sub-columns inside it.

What ranks inside a day

A day holds a piece of thinking: a goal, the decisions it forced, the options weighed, what somebody did, and what came back. That is the shape the chain already draws, and 80% of a day's edges stay inside it.

Rank by distance from whatever the day starts with, walking the edges rather than the types. A node nothing in the day points at opens the day, and every node sits one column right of the furthest thing pointing at it. Type ordering was the other candidate and it fails on the counts: observations are 224 of 565, so a type column holds two fifths of the graph and its edges reach everywhere.

Edges that cross days

127 of them, and they are the interesting minority. A claim reaching back to a goal from three weeks ago earns attention exactly because it is rare, and the longest spans thirteen columns.

They stay drawn. This is the one place the chain's lesson does not transfer: there, a long wire is noise from an arrangement that could have been better; here it is the fact worth seeing.

What the two drawings share

Most of what makes the chain readable has nothing to do with its ranks: a pane carrying what a node claims and its neighbours, selecting a node to light what it reaches, headings that stay put, bands that hold a group together, and a command that measures the result rather than asking somebody to squint at it.

All of that sits in one file that also computes the chain's own ranks. The shared half moves out, the same way provisioning a browser moved out of the test runner once a second caller wanted it. What stays behind on each side is the part that knows its own data: ranks from a vocabulary in one, days and local depth in the other.

What a reader does with twenty columns

A day reads on its own, which is the point. Today's 42 nodes are legible; 565 at once never will be, whatever the layout.

So the drawing keeps the horizontal scrolling the chain already has, and a day is the unit somebody navigates to. Nothing here collapses or hides a day — that is a filter, and a filter is worth building once the arrangement stops being the problem.

The canon chain keeps its ranks. Its data is genuinely ranked and time says nothing about it, so nothing here changes what it draws.

No filtering, no collapsing, no search. Twenty columns of days is more than one screen holds, and the answer to that is a filter, which earns its own pass once the arrangement is settled.

Nothing changes about what a knowledge node holds. This is a drawing, and the eight relations, the six types and the dated filename all stay as they are.

What opens a day that starts in the middle of something. A node whose only edges reach back to earlier days has nothing inside the day pointing at it, so it opens the day by the rule above, and several such nodes open it together. Whether that reads as a start or as a pile is a question for looking at it.

Whether a day with two unrelated pieces of thinking should say so. Bands carried subsystems on the chain page, and the equivalent here is the root goal a node traces back to, which would split a day into stripes the way a subsystem splits a column.

Whether the measurement transfers. Wires over boxes counted something the chain could act on; here the long edges stay by design, so the number would report a cost nobody intends to pay down.
